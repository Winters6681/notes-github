Safe when the system deviates

Why robustness

A safety certificate covers the model, not the machine

From power grids and autonomous vehicles to medical devices, safety-critical systems have become an inseparable part of our lives. When they fail, the consequences are severe and often irreversible, so safety is their top priority. To guarantee it, engineers verify systems formally before they are deployed. One such method is supervisory control theory (SCT): from a model of the system and a specification of what must never happen, it synthesizes a controller that is correct by construction.

Even with safety certification, accidents still happened.

1985–1987

Therac-25

A radiation therapy machine overdosed patients, some fatally, because of a software race condition triggered by the operator's input sequence (Leveson and Turner).

2018–2019

2018

A self-driving test car struck and killed a pedestrian in Tempe, Arizona, after its perception system failed to respond correctly.

These accidents point to something verification alone misses. A safety certificate is issued for the model that was verified. The real system can deviate from that model: hardware malfunctions, sensors are noisy, and the assumptions made while modeling do not always hold. The Therac-25 design, for example, assumed operators would follow a certain sequence of actions. Human actions are unpredictable, and sequences outside that assumption led to fatalities.

To ensure a system is safe, verification has to account for the ways it can deviate from its model. This is the question of robustness, and it is where my research lies.

Robustness in supervisory control / Neurosymbolic robustness analysis

Which deviations a controller is robust against?

Two cranes operating safely. Try to cause an accident.

Two cranes are at a construction site. Their task is to lift and place materials. The mobile crane can lift 5 tonnes. The tower crane can lift 18 tonnes. There are three types of material: bricks, steel beams and concrete slabs. Bricks weigh 2 tonnes and a beam weighs 5 tonnes. A concrete slab weighs 8 tonnes, which would tip the mobile crane over.

  • Safety ruleWhen a material is over the mobile crane's capacity, the tower crane is called in to lift it.
  • Safety ruleWhile the tower crane is operating, the mobile crane stands still, so that no collision can happen.

A safety controller enforces these rules:

  1. no materials are dropped from the sky,
  2. the cranes operate within their capacity,
  3. the cranes do not collide.

If everything goes by design, no accident is possible.

Have we seen all the possible accidents?

Each glitch can happen on its own, or it can happen together with other glitches. The crane example has 4 individual glitches. If we check each and every combination of glitches, that is 24 = 16 combinations to check. In the terms of this paper, that is 24 deviations.

    In real life, some deviations are impossible. This paper uses a large language model to set aside the ones that are not physically feasible. We then check whether a deviation can cause an accident. The largest set, or sets, of deviations that cause no accident is the robustness of the controller with respect to this system. With our approach, every physically feasible deviation is checked for accidents. The analysis tells us how safe our system is with the controller.

    In this paper: a language model proposes, model checking decides

    With Jonghan Lim, Ilya Kovalenko and Rômulo Meira-Góes · WODES 2026

    In this paper, I proposed a neurosymbolic framework. A large language model reads the system models, plain-language descriptions and rules of physics, and proposes the deviations that could really happen. Model checking then computes exactly which combinations of those deviations the controller survives. The language model only chooses what to check, so the robustness result keeps its formal guarantee.

    The crane site above is an illustration I made for this page. The paper's own case studies are a manufacturing cell, the Therac-25 interface and a communication protocol, shown below.

    Data-driven supervisory control

    When there is no model, only data

    With Ilya Kovalenko and Rômulo Meira-Góes · in preparation

    Building an accurate model of a real system is slow and labor-intensive, and sometimes it is not possible at all. What engineers often do have is data: logs of how the system has behaved. Many different models can explain the same logs, and the true system is only one of them.

    I am working on synthesizing supervisors directly from such data. The goal is a supervisor that is safe for every system the data could describe, while staying as permissive as the data allow. Seen this way, learning from data becomes another robustness problem: the uncertainty is no longer a fault in a known model, but everything the data cannot rule out.

    InputRecorded behaviorwhat the system did, and what it can never do

    Looking ahead

    From theory to working demonstrations

    My next step is to bring the theory and the hardware together: take supervisors synthesized with formal guarantees and run them on real and simulated robots, so that their robustness can be seen, tested and measured. In our lab, that means building on a range of tools.

    Manipulation

    UFactory Lite 6 robot arm

    A six-axis desktop arm for pick-and-place tasks, where supervisors decide which motions are allowed and when.

    Simulation

    NVIDIA Isaac Sim

    Digital twins to test supervisors and inject deviations safely before anything runs on hardware.

    Fabrication

    H2D 3D printer

    Customization for the testing environment.

    • Demonstrate robust and data-driven supervisors on physical robots, not only in models.
    • Use simulation to inject deviations and compare predicted robustness with observed behavior.
    • Scale the methods toward full manufacturing systems that combine arms, mobile robots and cameras.
